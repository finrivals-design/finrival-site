The anatomy of a candle

A line chart joins one price per period, usually the close. A candlestick shows four prices for each period in a single shape:

  • The thick part, the body, runs from the opening price to the closing price.
  • The thin lines above and below, the wicks or shadows, reach the highest and lowest prices of the period.
  • If the close is above the open, the candle is an up candle, usually green. If the close is below the open, it is a down candle, usually red.

Reading a single candle

The shape tells a short story about the period:

  • A long body, short wicks: the price moved firmly in one direction and stayed there.
  • A small body, long wicks: the price swung a lot but finished near where it started. Buyers and sellers were undecided.
  • A long lower wick: the price fell during the period but recovered before the close.
  • A long upper wick: the price rose during the period but gave back most of the gain.

Reading a run of candles

One candle shows one period; a chart of many shows how the price has behaved over time. A series of higher closes suggests an upward trend; a series of lower closes, a downward one. Large candles mean big daily swings, which is a sign of higher volatility.

Traders give names to many candle patterns. They can be a useful vocabulary, but no pattern predicts the next move reliably. A chart describes what already happened; it does not know what news comes tomorrow.

Daily, hourly and one-minute candles

The same company looks different at different timeframes. A daily chart, where each candle is one trading day, shows the bigger picture over weeks and months. A one-minute chart shows the noise inside a single day. Most beginners learn more from daily candles first.

Note that some longer ranges are drawn as a line instead of candles. When a chart covers many years, showing every daily candle would be unreadable, so the line follows the closing prices.
