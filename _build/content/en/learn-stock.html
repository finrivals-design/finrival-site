What you own when you own a share

A company like Apple or Nvidia is split into a very large number of equal pieces called shares. When you buy one share, you own one of those pieces. If a company has 10 billion shares and you hold 10, you own one billionth of it.

That ownership is real. Shareholders are entitled to a part of the company's profits when it pays them out, and in most companies they can vote on important decisions such as electing the board. In practice, a small shareholder's vote is tiny, but the principle is the same as for the largest investor.

"Stock" and "share" are often used to mean the same thing. Strictly, a stock is ownership in companies in general, and a share is one unit of it. When Finrival lists a stock, it means the shares of one company that trade on an exchange.

Why companies sell shares

Companies sell shares to raise money without borrowing it. The first time a company sells shares to the public is called an IPO, an initial public offering. After that, the shares trade between investors on an exchange such as the New York Stock Exchange or Nasdaq. When you buy a share later, you almost always buy it from another investor, not from the company.

Why the price moves every day

An exchange is a market. At any moment some people want to buy a share and others want to sell it. The price is simply the level at which the last buyer and seller agreed. If more people want to buy than sell, the price rises until enough sellers appear. If more want to sell, it falls.

What makes people want to buy or sell? Mostly their expectations about the company's future profits. Typical triggers include:

  • Earnings reports: a company reports higher or lower profit than investors expected.
  • News: a new product, a lawsuit, a contract, a change of chief executive.
  • The wider economy: interest rates, inflation and growth change what all companies are worth.
  • Sector moves: news about one chip maker often moves other chip makers too.

Sometimes a price moves with no clear reason at all. Markets react to rumours, to large investors adjusting their positions and to plain mood. That is why Finrival never presents a price move as certain to continue.

How shares can pay you

There are two ways a shareholder can benefit:

  • Price change: if you buy at $50 and the price later reaches $60, your share is worth $10 more. The gain is only "real" when you sell; until then it can disappear.
  • Dividends: some companies pay part of their profit to shareholders, usually every quarter. Many growing companies pay none and reinvest instead.

The price can also fall. A share can lose most of its value, and if a company fails, shareholders are among the last to be paid and often receive nothing. That risk is the other side of the possible reward.

The closing price

US exchanges trade from 9:30 to 16:00 New York time on weekdays. The last price of the regular session is the closing price, and it is the number most often used to describe "how a stock did today".
